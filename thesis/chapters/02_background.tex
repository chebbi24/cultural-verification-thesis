\chapter{Background and Foundations}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter introduces the concepts required to understand the evaluation problem addressed in this thesis. It deliberately separates foundational concepts from the comparison of individual cultural-evaluation systems in \cref{ch:related}. The focus is therefore not on cataloguing prior work, but on establishing the technical and conceptual vocabulary needed for the proposed verifier and its later empirical comparison.

\section{Large Language Models and Alignment}

Large language models (LLMs) are typically trained first to model distributions over natural-language sequences and are subsequently adapted so that their outputs better satisfy user instructions and desired behavioral constraints. Modern post-training therefore adds an alignment stage to raw language modeling. A widely used formulation is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), in which human demonstrations and preference comparisons provide supervision for instruction following and preference optimization. In the InstructGPT pipeline, for example, supervised demonstrations are followed by a reward model trained on ranked model outputs and a reinforcement-learning stage that optimizes the policy against that learned reward signal \parencite{ouyang2022instructgpt}. The central point for this thesis is that alignment is mediated by operational signals: demonstrations, preferences, policies, constitutions, or evaluators define what behavior is rewarded.

Such signals are necessarily incomplete proxies for the full range of judgments that may matter in deployment. A model can be generally helpful and still provide a response that is poorly adapted to the cultural, institutional, linguistic, or interpersonal situation described by a user. Conversely, a response that differs from a majority preference is not automatically culturally inappropriate. Cultural evaluation therefore raises a pluralism problem in addition to a general quality problem. \textcite{sorensen2024pluralistic} distinguish several forms of pluralistic alignment and argue that systems intended to serve diverse populations may need to represent, steer among, or calibrate to multiple reasonable perspectives rather than collapse them into a single undifferentiated preference. This observation is important for the present work because cultural appropriateness is treated as context-sensitive and potentially contested, not as a universal scalar property that can be inferred from one population-wide preference signal.

The thesis consequently uses the term \emph{alignment} in a deliberately narrow and operational sense. A culturally aligned response is not assumed to encode an objectively correct culture. Rather, it is a response that is appropriately adapted to the explicit situation, does not materially misrepresent relevant practices or constraints, and represents legitimate variation where the context does not justify a single categorical answer. This distinction is developed further in \cref{sec:cultural-appropriateness} and operationalized in the D01--D10 framework in \cref{ch:framework}.

\section{Preference Alignment and Reward Models}
\label{sec:background-rm}

Reward models (RMs) provide one mechanism for turning preference data into an evaluative signal. In a common setup, the model receives a prompt and one or more candidate responses and estimates which response is preferred. Classifier-based RMs often produce a scalar reward for each prompt--response pair, while generative evaluators can produce judgments or scores through language generation. During training, pairwise chosen/rejected examples can teach a model to assign higher reward to responses that better match the preference distribution represented in the data. During inference or evaluation, the same score can be used to rank several candidate responses.

This mechanism is useful because it transforms costly human comparisons into a reusable learned evaluator. General RM benchmarks therefore test whether a reward model can discriminate preferred from rejected responses across heterogeneous tasks. RewardBench, for example, evaluates reward models on preference discrimination rather than on cultural evidence gathering \parencite{lambert2025rewardbench}. The Skywork-Reward-V2 family used later in this thesis is likewise a general preference-model family developed through large-scale preference-data curation \parencite{liu2026skyworkrewardv2}. These systems are therefore suitable as independent baselines for asking whether a general preference model happens to select the same candidate that human annotators judge most culturally appropriate.

The interpretation of a reward score nevertheless requires care. First, a scalar reward is a model-dependent preference signal rather than a direct measurement of factual or cultural truth. Second, the score does not by itself reveal which cultural claim, social norm, linguistic choice, institutional requirement, or omission caused the ranking. Third, a reward model may have learned correlations that work on the training distribution without representing the causal cultural distinction that a benchmark intends to test. These concerns do not make reward modeling inappropriate; they define the question against which a more diagnostic evaluator should be compared.

For this reason, the reward model in this thesis is kept strictly separate from the proposed verifier. It receives prompt--response pairs and produces independent scores; those scores never enter evidence retrieval, cultural dimension scoring, or the verifier's ranking. This separation is methodological rather than rhetorical. If the verifier incorporated the RM score, a later comparison between the two would no longer distinguish preference-based evaluation from evidence-grounded verification.

The distinction between preference and evidence also affects tie handling and uncertainty. A conventional ranking implementation may always return one maximum-scoring candidate even when two raw scores are exactly equal. By contrast, the verifier developed in this thesis explicitly represents an exact tie as \texttt{no\_clear\_winner}. The experimental methodology therefore has to define baseline tie behavior before final evaluation rather than implicitly interpreting an arbitrary implementation detail as a meaningful preference. The baseline design is discussed in \cref{ch:experiment}.

\section{LLM-as-a-Judge}
\label{sec:background-judge}

A second evaluation paradigm uses an instruction-tuned LLM directly as a judge. Instead of learning a separate scalar reward head, the evaluator is prompted with one or more responses and an evaluation instruction, and it returns a preference, rating, or rationale. This approach is attractive because it is flexible, inexpensive relative to repeated human assessment, and can express task-specific criteria in natural language. MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena helped establish this paradigm and showed that strong LLM judges can exhibit substantial agreement with human preferences in some general assistant-evaluation settings \parencite{zheng2023llmjudge}.

However, direct LLM judging is not measurement-free. \textcite{zheng2023llmjudge} report systematic evaluator tendencies including position bias, verbosity bias, and self-enhancement effects. \textcite{wang2024notfairevaluators} further show that response ordering can materially affect comparative judgments. These findings matter for cultural evaluation because a judge may be sensitive to surface presentation, response length, model style, or ordering independently of whether the cultural content is well grounded. They therefore motivate explicit controls such as frozen prompts, stable ordering procedures, and separate reporting of judge behavior.

The present thesis treats direct LLM judging as a distinct comparator rather than as the verifier itself. A direct judge sees the candidate responses and is asked to decide among them. The proposed evidence-grounded verifier instead separates evidence acquisition from the final candidate comparison: once verification questions have been generated, the evidence stage cannot receive the candidate text or target object. This architectural choice is intended to reduce one class of candidate-conditioned evaluation effects, but the judge-bias literature does not prove that this design removes bias. In particular, the verification questions themselves originate from candidate targets and may carry framing information. The final experiment must therefore establish the empirical consequences rather than infer them from architecture alone.

\section{Cultural Appropriateness and Cultural Alignment}
\label{sec:cultural-appropriateness}

The phrase \emph{cultural correctness} suggests that a single response can be classified against a fixed, homogeneous cultural truth. That assumption is too strong for many real-world situations. Institutional definitions of culture are deliberately broader. UNESCO describes culture in terms that include material, intellectual and emotional features as well as lifestyles, ways of living together, value systems, traditions and beliefs \parencite{unesco2001culturaldiversity}. The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights similarly treats cultural life as broad, evolving and connected to identity, language, knowledge, customs, traditions and participation \parencite{cescr2009gc21}. These definitions are not computational taxonomies, but they establish two important premises: culture is multi-faceted, and cultural membership does not imply one static set of rules.

Recent NLP surveys reach a related methodological conclusion. Reviewing more than ninety studies, \textcite{adilazuarda2024culture} observe that much work measures selected \emph{proxies} of culture rather than a universally agreed definition of culture, and that important semantic facets remain unevenly covered. \textcite{liu2025culturallyaware} likewise identify the lack of a common understanding of culture as a central challenge for culturally aware and adapted NLP, while proposing a finer-grained taxonomy to organize existing work. A broader survey by \textcite{pawar2025culturalawareness} similarly frames cultural awareness as extending beyond multilinguality and surveys text and multimodal approaches, dataset construction, adaptation, benchmarking and ethical concerns. These surveys motivate the decision in this thesis to state an explicit operationalization instead of treating the word ``culture'' as self-defining.

Accordingly, this thesis uses \emph{cultural appropriateness} for a context-sensitive evaluative construct. The aim is to determine whether a response is suitable for the explicit cultural and situational context while distinguishing different epistemic kinds of claims. Several distinctions are particularly important.

\paragraph{Descriptive practice and tendency.} A response may describe a practice that is common in a population without implying that every member follows it. Evidence for a tendency should therefore not be restated as a universal rule. Cross-national opinion work illustrates why this matters: population-level distributions can differ across countries while still containing substantial within-country variation \parencite{durmus2023globalopinionqa,tao2024culturalbias}.

\paragraph{Social norm and etiquette.} A social expectation can be context-sensitive even when it is widely recognized. NormAd explicitly studies adaptation from abstract values to more concrete social norms and evaluates whether models respond appropriately as contextual specificity changes \parencite{rao2025normad}. A useful evaluator must therefore attend to role, relationship, locale and situation rather than merely retrieve a country label.

\paragraph{Pragmatic convention.} Linguistic appropriateness is not exhausted by grammaticality or translation. Register, forms of address, idioms, implicature and conversational expectations can alter what a response communicates in a given interaction. Cultural-linguistic evaluation is therefore conceptually distinct from factual cultural knowledge, even though the two can overlap.

\paragraph{Value and moral perspective.} Values are often plural and contested. GlobalOpinionQA demonstrates that subjective opinion distributions vary across populations and that prompting models to adopt national perspectives can shift outputs while also introducing stereotypical behavior \parencite{durmus2023globalopinionqa}. A culturally appropriate response should therefore not convert a population tendency into moral unanimity. This thesis treats value pluralism as part of the object being evaluated rather than noise to be averaged away.

\paragraph{Law, policy and institutional rule.} A binding legal or institutional constraint is epistemically different from etiquette. SafeWorld explicitly combines legal policies and cultural norms in geo-diverse safety scenarios, making the distinction operationally relevant \parencite{yin2024safeworld}. A response that presents an informal custom as law, or a legal requirement as optional etiquette, can be culturally contextual yet still materially misleading.

These distinctions lead to a general principle used throughout the thesis: an evaluator should match the strength and type of its conclusion to the strength and type of the available evidence. Cultural appropriateness can therefore require positive adaptation, accurate qualification, or explicit acknowledgement of variation; it need not always produce one categorical cultural rule.

\section{Evidence-Grounded Evaluation and Retrieval}
\label{sec:background-retrieval}

Language models contain substantial parametric knowledge, but their internal knowledge is difficult to inspect and may be outdated, incomplete or uneven across cultures. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) introduced a general architecture in which a parametric model is combined with retrieved non-parametric information, allowing outputs to depend on explicitly retrieved material rather than on model parameters alone \parencite{lewis2020rag}. The verifier developed in this thesis does not use RAG to generate a better user response; it adopts the more general principle that external evidence can make an evaluative process more inspectable.

Evidence grounding is particularly relevant when a response contains claims about law, institutions, current policy, regional practice, or the prevalence of a cultural convention. In such cases, an evaluator that reasons only from its parameters risks turning unverified model knowledge into an apparently authoritative judgment. Retrieval creates an opportunity to attach a source, date, scope and provenance to the claim being assessed. It also makes disagreement visible: two sources may describe different subgroups, time periods, denominations, regions or survey distributions, in which case a good evidence synthesis should preserve that variation rather than average it into a universal statement.

Retrieval does not remove uncertainty. Search systems rank available documents, and availability itself is culturally uneven. High-resource languages and institutions tend to produce more accessible web material than low-resource communities. A retrieved excerpt can omit qualifications contained elsewhere on the page. Source authority is also question-dependent: an official legal source is highly appropriate for statutory requirements, while community or insider sources may be more informative for lived practice. For this reason, the verifier's source classes are used as contextual descriptors rather than as a universal numeric hierarchy.

Provenance controls are also necessary because retrieval can compromise evaluation validity when the search process encounters benchmark answers, annotations, or derived result files. Benchmark contamination is a broader concern in LLM evaluation; \textcite{deng2024contamination} demonstrate that contamination can affect modern benchmark measurements and motivate explicit contamination analysis. In this thesis, known repository and annotation paths are excluded from retrieval and the evidence used by a run is recorded. Such controls reduce known leakage channels but cannot establish that every mirror, copied page, or pretraining exposure has been eliminated.

\section{Evaluation Concepts}
\label{sec:background-evaluation}

The final empirical study compares three machine evaluators with human annotations. Several measurement concepts must therefore be kept distinct.

\paragraph{Reference judgment and ground truth.} Human annotation provides a reference against which evaluator choices can be compared, but cultural judgments are not assumed to constitute objective universal truth. With multiple annotators, agreement itself is informative. A clear majority for one candidate, a majority for ``no clear winner,'' and an unresolved split represent different empirical outcomes and should not be collapsed into the same label. The experimental protocol in \cref{ch:experiment} therefore freezes the aggregation rule only after the realized annotation structure is inspected.

\paragraph{Agreement and correctness.} If the aggregated human label is used as the study's reference, system-level ``accuracy'' means agreement with that operational reference under the stated protocol. It does not imply philosophical or anthropological correctness. This terminology is especially important when annotators disagree or when the sample is small.

\paragraph{Abstention and coverage.} An evaluator may be unable to support a judgment. Selective-classification research formalizes the broader idea that a system can reject some predictions in exchange for greater reliability on the retained set \parencite{geifman2017selective}. The verifier's abstention mechanism is only an architectural analogue of this reject option; it does not implement the selective-classification algorithm or inherit its statistical risk guarantees. Consequently, abstention rate and scoring coverage must be reported alongside agreement. A method that scores only easy cases should not be compared with a method that always returns a winner without making coverage visible.

\paragraph{Confidence and uncertainty.} Human confidence and model confidence are different variables. Annotator confidence can reveal ambiguous prompts or unstable reference labels; evidence sufficiency can reveal whether retrieved material supports a machine judgment. Neither is automatically calibrated as a probability of correctness. The final analysis therefore treats confidence as an empirical signal to be analyzed rather than a substitute for agreement.

\paragraph{Paired comparison.} Because the same Best-of-4 items are evaluated by the verifier, reward model and direct judge, system comparisons are paired at the item level. Statistical analysis should exploit that pairing rather than compare unrelated aggregate percentages. The exact tests and confidence intervals are selected in \cref{ch:experiment} once the final outcome structure is known.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Reproducibility requires more than fixing the model temperature. Model versions, prompts, retrieval dates, evidence, ordering, source filters and code revisions can all alter evaluation outcomes. This thesis therefore distinguishes LIVE evidence acquisition from REPLAY evaluation over frozen retrieval artifacts. REPLAY controls evidence drift but does not make semantic LLM calls mathematically deterministic. That boundary is a methodological property of the implementation, not an empirical result.

Taken together, these foundations motivate the central design problem of this thesis. Preference models and direct judges provide scalable comparison signals, but cultural appropriateness can depend on contextual distinctions, plural values, external facts and current institutional constraints that are difficult to represent in a single opaque score. The next chapter examines how existing cultural benchmarks and alignment methods address parts of this problem and identifies the specific gap targeted by the proposed evidence-grounded verifier.
