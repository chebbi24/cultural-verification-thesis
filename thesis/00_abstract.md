# Abstract

Large language models are increasingly used for information, writing support, recommendations and everyday advice across different countries and communities. A response can be fluent and useful at first sight while still being culturally misleading. It may generalise one local practice to a whole population, confuse a social custom with a legal rule, use an inappropriate form of address, ignore religious or historical sensitivities, or reduce a diverse group to a stereotype. These problems are difficult to evaluate because cultural appropriateness is not a single factual property. It depends on the stated context and often includes legitimate variation within the same culture.

This thesis presents **Vericult**, a standalone framework for the post-hoc verification of culturally situated LLM responses. Vericult receives a user prompt and one generated response. It first extracts only explicit context from the prompt and decides which of ten cultural dimensions are relevant. It then identifies a small number of material response targets. Externally checkable claims, descriptions of cultural norms and context-dependent recommendations are converted into neutral verification questions. Evidence is retrieved independently from the final candidate comparison, filtered for benchmark leakage, classified by source type, and summarised into a bounded evidence memo. Only after this evidence has been frozen is the response compared with it. The final output contains dimension-level scores, an overall cultural-appropriateness decision, evidence coverage, abstentions and a trace of the intermediate steps.

The final experiment uses **360 fixed prompt-response pairs** from three complementary corpora: 120 custom cultural challenge prompts, 120 prompts from six external multicultural datasets, and 120 externally grounded red-team prompts. All responses are generated with the same frozen `gpt-oss:120b-mxfp4` model. Vericult uses `vllm/qwen3.6:35b-a3b-fp8` as its semantic backbone and Tavily for web retrieval. Evidence is first collected in LIVE mode, audited and frozen, and then reused in REPLAY mode. REPLAY is the primary Vericult result because it separates the final semantic evaluation from changes in live web search.

The empirical results are reported without treating corpus membership as a ground-truth cultural label. The final analysis examines the distribution of Vericult outcomes, dimension scores, evidence sufficiency, abstention, retrieval behaviour, system failures and trace-backed examples. It also compares Vericult with an independent reward-model baseline and a direct LLM judge where comparable single-response outputs are available. The earlier five-annotator Best-of-4 study is retained only as development evidence because its labels refer to different model responses and cannot be transferred to the final experiment.

The main contribution of this thesis is therefore not a new cultural knowledge benchmark alone, but an inspectable verification process. Vericult makes explicit what cultural aspect is being judged, what evidence is used, where evidence is weak, and where the system abstains. The study evaluates whether this design provides a useful and reproducible basis for cultural verification while also documenting the limits of evidence-grounded automated judgment.
