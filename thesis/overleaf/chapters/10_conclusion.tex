\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Summary}

This thesis studies a simple but difficult question: how can one generated response be checked for cultural appropriateness in a way that is easier to inspect than a single score?

I developed Vericult as a post-hoc verifier. It does not change the generator. Instead, it reads the prompt, selects the relevant cultural dimensions, identifies a small number of important response targets, retrieves evidence for the targets that can be checked externally, freezes that evidence, and only then compares the response with it.

The method also allows abstention. This matters because not every cultural issue can be settled by a web search. Some questions are factual, while others depend on values, context, or variation inside a community.

The final evaluation uses 360 frozen prompt--response pairs: 120 custom PLT red-team prompts, 120 prompts from public cultural datasets, and 120 externally grounded red-team prompts. Every response is generated with the same GPT-OSS model. Vericult is evaluated independently from the generator.

\section{What Can Be Claimed}

Even before the final numbers are inserted, the scope of the claim is clear. Vericult provides an inspectable evaluation process. It separates prompt context, evidence collection, and final scoring, and it stores the intermediate decisions needed for later review.

What the thesis cannot claim without the final evidence is equally clear. It cannot say that Vericult is more accurate than a reward model simply because the systems disagree. It cannot treat the old Best-of-4 human study as gold data for the new GPT-OSS responses. It also cannot claim that retrieved web evidence gives one objective answer for every cultural question.

\section{Future Work}

\subsection{Human Evaluation of the Exact Final Responses}

The strongest next step would be a new human study on the same 360 responses used in the final experiment. The annotators should see the exact frozen responses and should be allowed to express uncertainty or disagreement. This would make direct accuracy comparisons much stronger.

\subsection{Other Verifier Backbones}

The current study uses one semantic backbone. Running the same frozen evidence and rubric with other strong models would show how much the final judgment depends on that choice.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

A future study could remove individual parts of the pipeline, such as the variation question, the evidence freeze, or the abstention rule. This would show which parts actually change the quality of the final judgment.

\subsection{Better Multilingual Retrieval}

Some cultural information is easier to find in the local language than in English. Stronger multilingual retrieval could improve evidence coverage, especially for local customs and minority perspectives.

\subsection{Community-Aware Evidence}

Official sources are useful for law and institutions, but they do not always represent lived experience. Future versions could make better use of community sources while still checking provenance and reliability.

\subsection{Question-Neutrality Checks}

The evidence stage is blind only after the questions have been generated. A poorly written question can still carry the response's framing into retrieval. An additional neutrality check could reduce this risk.

\section{Closing Remarks}

Cultural appropriateness is more than memorising customs. A useful response has to handle context, variation, language, institutions, values, history, and identity without turning one group's experience into a universal rule.

Vericult is one attempt to make that evaluation process visible. Its value should be judged from the final results and failure cases, not from the architecture alone.
