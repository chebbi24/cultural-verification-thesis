\chapter{Background and Foundations}
\label{chap:background}

\section{Language Models and Alignment}

A large language model generates text by predicting likely continuations from its training and post-training data. Modern models are usually adapted so that they follow instructions and produce responses that people prefer. One well-known method is reinforcement learning from human feedback \parencite{ouyang2022training}.

This thesis does not train a new language model. The generator is fixed before the final evaluation starts. The main question is what happens after a response has already been produced.

\section{Reward Models}

A reward model gives a numerical score to a prompt--response pair. In preference training, the score can be used to rank responses or guide optimisation. For evaluation, a higher score often means that the model considers one response better than another.

The weakness for this thesis is that the score is hard to interpret culturally. A reward value does not show whether the model objected to a stereotype, a wrong historical claim, an unsuitable form of address, or something unrelated to culture.

Skywork is therefore kept outside Vericult. It is used only as an independent comparison system in the final experiment.

\section{LLM-as-a-Judge}

A direct LLM judge receives an evaluation instruction together with the response. It can return a label, score, or explanation. This is simple to implement and often useful.

However, the judge normally relies on its own internal knowledge. It may miss a factual error or give a confident explanation that cannot be checked easily. Research on LLM judges also reports evaluator effects such as order sensitivity in pairwise comparisons \parencite{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair}.

In the final study, the direct judge is another external baseline. It is not part of the Vericult pipeline.

\section{What Cultural Appropriateness Means Here}

I use the term \emph{cultural appropriateness} for the way a response handles the cultural situation stated in the prompt. This is different from asking whether the response follows the majority custom.

A person can choose not to follow a common tradition. A minority view can also be legitimate. The verifier should therefore distinguish between a practice being common, expected, legally required, or personally preferred.

This is why the thesis avoids the older phrase \emph{cultural correctness}. That phrase sounds as if every cultural question has one fixed answer. In many cases, this is not true.

\section{Four Types of Cultural Claims}

The verifier repeatedly needs to separate four kinds of statements.

\subsection{Descriptive Practice}

A descriptive practice says what people often do. For example, a source may report that a greeting or food tradition is common in a region. This kind of evidence describes a pattern, not an obligation.

\subsection{Social Norm}

A social norm concerns what is usually expected in an interaction. It may involve greetings, gift giving, punctuality, dress, or forms of address. Norms can vary by age, region, institution, or relationship.

\subsection{Formal Rule}

A formal rule comes from law, policy, or an institution. It should not be presented as if it were simply a cultural preference. The reverse mistake is also possible: a custom should not be described as legally required when it is not.

\subsection{Value Statement}

A value statement concerns ideas such as autonomy, family duty, fairness, authority, or religion. External sources can document different views, but they do not always prove that one value order is universally correct.

These four types are useful later because they affect how much external evidence can help.

\section{Evidence-Grounded Evaluation}

Retrieval-augmented generation usually brings external documents into the generation process \parencite{lewis2020rag}. Vericult uses retrieval in another way. The response is already fixed. Retrieval is used only to check selected parts of that response.

For a factual target, the evidence may answer a clear question. For a cultural norm, the evidence should also show scope and variation. For a value conflict, the role of evidence is more limited.

Missing evidence is not treated as proof that the response is wrong. The system can keep the target unresolved.

\section{Abstention}

Abstention is used when the verifier does not have enough basis for a score. This is different from assigning a low score. A low score means that the response is judged to be misaligned with the rubric. Abstention means that the system cannot justify such a judgment.

This distinction is important in cultural evaluation because source coverage is uneven. Some legal rules are documented clearly. Local practices, minority perspectives, or informal social expectations may be much harder to find online.

\section{LIVE and REPLAY}

External search changes over time, so a final result based on live web evidence is difficult to reproduce exactly. Vericult deals with this through two modes.

In \textbf{LIVE} mode, the system performs the search and stores the retrieved material. The stored evidence is then audited and hashed.

In \textbf{REPLAY} mode, the verifier uses the saved evidence instead of searching again. REPLAY therefore fixes the evidence basis used for the final analysis.

This does not make the semantic model perfectly deterministic. It only prevents the external evidence from changing between the main analysis and later inspection.

\section{Evaluation Terms Used Later}

Several terms appear throughout the experiment:

\begin{description}
\item[Assessable response] A response with enough substantive content to evaluate.
\item[Material target] A response span that could change the final cultural judgment.
\item[Retrievable target] A target for which external evidence is useful and appropriate.
\item[Evidence memo] A structured summary of the retrieved support, variation, and remaining uncertainty.
\item[Abstention] A dimension that is left unresolved because the basis is insufficient.
\item[Trace] The stored record of intermediate decisions, model calls, questions, evidence, and scores.
\end{description}

These concepts are used in the method described in Chapter~\ref{chap:verifier}.
