\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Large language models are used for travel advice, workplace communication, explanations of traditions, and many other everyday tasks. In these situations, factual correctness is only part of the problem. The answer also has to fit the situation.

A small example is enough to show why. The greeting Moin is common in northern Germany. A model may know the word and still give poor advice if it presents it as the natural choice in every German region. The same pattern appears in harder cases involving religion, family expectations, gender, history, identity, or law.

For this thesis, culture is therefore not the same as nationality. A prompt can involve a region, a religion, a language variety, a workplace role, a family relationship, or a historical issue. It would be risky to infer beliefs or behaviour from a person's name or passport alone.

The surveys by Adilazuarda et al. and Liu et al. show the same broad problem from the research side: cultural NLP covers many different signals and tasks rather than one single variable \parencite{adilazuarda2024culture,liu2025culturally}. My focus is narrower. I want to know whether one generated response handles the cultural situation in its prompt well.
\section{Why Existing Evaluation Is Not Enough}

There are already several ways to evaluate LLM outputs. Reward models assign preference scores. LLM judges can give direct ratings and explanations. Cultural benchmarks test knowledge or norm understanding. These approaches are useful, but they answer different questions.

A reward score can tell us that one response is preferred, but it usually does not tell us which cultural claim caused the problem or what source supports the judgment. A direct LLM judge gives more explanation, but it may still rely only on its own internal knowledge. Previous work has also shown that LLM judges can be affected by presentation and order effects \parencite{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair}.

Cultural benchmarks such as BLEND, CulturalBench, NormAd, SafeWorld, and CARB test important parts of cultural capability \parencite{myung2024blend,chiu2025culturalbench,rao2025normad,yin2024safeworld,zhang2025carb}. My task is different. The response already exists, and I want to check that response after generation.

That means the evaluator should show more than a final label. It should show which part of the answer was checked, what evidence was used, and where the system was unsure.
\section{Problem Statement}

The task in this thesis starts with one prompt $p$ and one fixed response $r$. The verifier has to decide whether culture is relevant, identify the parts of the response that matter, retrieve evidence when a claim can be checked externally, and then apply a clear rubric.

This is not ordinary fact checking. Some claims are factual, for example a legal rule or the date of a public holiday. Other questions are about etiquette, values, or identity and may not have one simple answer. Sources can also disagree or represent only one part of a community.

A second difficulty comes from retrieval itself. If the response wording is used directly as a search query, the system may mainly find material that repeats the same framing. For that reason, Vericult first turns the selected claim into neutral questions and only then starts the evidence search.

The main problem is practical: how can cultural evaluation be made more transparent without pretending that every cultural question has one objective answer?
\section{Research Gap}

Existing work covers many parts of this problem, but not the exact combination used here. CulturalBench and BLEND focus on cultural knowledge. NormAd studies social norms. SafeWorld studies geographically different safety and legal settings. CultureLLM changes the generator itself. CARB evaluates reward models.

Vericult is placed after generation. It checks one prompt--response pair and keeps separate records for context, targets, evidence, and scores. The main contribution is the order of these steps: important claims are selected, checkable claims are turned into neutral questions, evidence is gathered without the response text, the evidence is frozen, and only then is the response compared with it.

This is a design choice that can be tested. It is not a claim that Vericult is automatically better than a reward model or a direct judge.
\section{Research Questions}

The final thesis is organized around four research questions.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.7cm,style=nextline]

\item[RQ1] \textbf{What cultural-appropriateness outcomes does Vericult assign to the 360 fixed responses, and how often can it reach an assessable decision?}

This question studies the basic behavior of the complete verifier. It includes the frequencies of culturally appropriate, partially appropriate, inappropriate, insufficient-evidence, not-culturally-applicable, and not-assessable outcomes.

\item[RQ2] \textbf{How often does Vericult obtain sufficient external evidence for selected response targets, and how does evidence availability affect its decisions?}

This question focuses on the evidence pipeline. It examines target-level evidence coverage, conflicting evidence, insufficiency, and abstention.

\item[RQ3] \textbf{What errors and limitations can be identified by examining the verifier's traces, dimensions, abstentions, and retrieval results?}

This question uses the diagnostic information that a scalar evaluator does not expose. The goal is to identify whether mistakes begin in context extraction, planning, target selection, retrieval, evidence synthesis, target comparison, or scoring.

\item[RQ4] \textbf{Where a valid common reference is available, how do Vericult's judgments compare with an independent reward model and a direct LLM judge?}

This question places Vericult next to simpler or more established evaluator types. It is conditional because disagreement between automated systems does not tell us which system is correct.

\end{description}

The wording of the questions is non-directional. None of them assumes that Vericult will outperform the baselines.

\section{Contributions}

The thesis makes five main contributions.

\subsection{An Operational D01--D10 Cultural Framework}

The thesis develops a ten-dimensional rubric covering:

\begin{itemize}
\item everyday life and material culture;
\item language, discourse and pragmatics;
\item social etiquette and interpersonal norms;
\item values, ethics and moral pluralism;
\item law, policy and institutional rules;
\item religion, ritual and taboo;
\item family, kinship, gender and generations;
\item work, education and civic participation;
\item cultural heritage, history, arts and collective memory;
\item identity, diversity and intergroup relations.
\end{itemize}

The framework is literature-grounded but operational. It is not presented as a universal theory of culture.

\subsection{A Standalone Evidence-Grounded Verifier}

Vericult takes a fixed prompt and response and evaluates them independently from the generator. It separates prompt context, dimension planning, material targets, neutral verification questions, external evidence, frozen memos, target comparison, and final scoring.

\subsection{A Candidate-Blind Evidence Boundary}

After neutral questions have been generated, the evidence engine no longer receives the response or target object. It receives only the questions and validated prompt context.

This does not eliminate search-engine bias, and the question generator can still transmit framing. It does provide a concrete structural separation between evidence gathering and response comparison.

\subsection{Explicit Uncertainty and Reproducibility}

The verifier can abstain when the basis is insufficient. LIVE retrieval artifacts are stored, audited, and then reused in REPLAY.

The final thesis reports REPLAY as the primary Vericult execution because it uses a fixed external evidence basis.

\subsection{A Controlled 360-Pair Evaluation}

The final experiment contains:

\begin{itemize}
\item 120 PLT prompts;
\item 120 prompts selected from six external cultural datasets;
\item 120 externally grounded red-team prompts.
\end{itemize}

Every prompt receives one response from the same frozen GPT-OSS generator. This avoids mixing final responses from different model sources.

\section{Development History and Final Scope}

The project went through several designs before the final experiment.

The first version used Best-of-4 ranking on 30 PLT prompts. Four responses were compared for each prompt, and five human annotators selected the most culturally appropriate candidate.

That stage was useful for developing the rubric, target extraction, ranking behavior, and annotation workflow. It also showed an important limitation: the main goal of the project is not necessarily to choose the best of four answers. A real user may already have only one answer that needs verification.

The supervisor also emphasized that the verifier should be evaluated on responses from a stronger and consistent generator and that the verifier backbone should be strong enough for the task.

The final design changed in three ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Best-of-4 is replaced by one response per prompt.
\item All final responses are regenerated with one fixed GPT-OSS model.
\item The final Vericult semantic backbone is Qwen3.6 35B-A3B through L3S.
\end{enumerate}

The old human labels remain in the repository for development provenance but are not reused as labels for the new responses.

\section{Innovation Boundary}

The contribution should also be read with a clear boundary. Vericult does not train a new cultural language model, does not replace human cultural expertise, and does not claim to discover a universally correct culture label. It is an evaluation layer placed after generation. Its scientific question is whether evidence-grounded decomposition and explicit uncertainty make cultural assessment more transparent and diagnostically useful.

This boundary is important when interpreting later comparisons. A reward model can be useful even though it does not expose source evidence, and a direct LLM judge can be correct without retrieval. Vericult's distinctive claim is not that these systems are invalid; it is that culturally situated judgments benefit from an architecture in which factual support, contextual variation, and the final normative assessment can be inspected separately.

Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes the work, answers the research questions using the final results, and gives directions for future work.
