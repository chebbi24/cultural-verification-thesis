\chapter{Related Work and Research Gap}
\label{chap:related}

\section{What Previous Research Actually Measures}

Cultural evaluation is not a single benchmark task. Some papers test knowledge of local customs, some test whether a model can recognize the acceptability of an action, and others study whether preferences or reward scores change across languages. These tasks are related, but they cannot be used interchangeably. A model can answer a factual multiple-choice question correctly and still produce inappropriate advice in a longer conversation.

Two surveys are useful starting points. Adilazuarda et al.\ review work on cultural representation in LLMs and describe how researchers use demographic and semantic proxies to represent culture \parencite{adilazuarda2024culture}. Liu et al.\ propose a detailed taxonomy for culturally aware and adapted NLP, arguing that the field lacks one shared operational definition of culture \parencite{liu2025culturally}. This thesis does not claim that the D01--D10 rubric is a new universal definition. It is an engineering framework designed to organize response-level errors.

\section{Everyday Cultural Knowledge: BLEND}

BLEND is a benchmark of everyday cultural knowledge across different languages and regions \parencite{myung2024blend}. Its motivation is practical: information about ordinary celebrations, foods, school activities, or habits may be familiar to local people but under-represented in the web documents used for model training. Its questions make cultural knowledge measurable, but they do not necessarily show how a model uses that knowledge in a real user response.

For Vericult, the main lesson is that seemingly simple daily-life advice can depend on location and social context. This motivates D01 (everyday life and material culture) and the decision to retrieve external evidence only when the response contains a checkable statement. BLEND results are not used as Vericult results.

\section{Challenging Cultural Knowledge: CulturalBench}

CulturalBench contains 1,696 human-written and human-verified questions from 45 regions and 17 topics in its published ACL 2025 version \parencite{chiu2025culturalbench}. Five independent annotators verified each question. The paper reports 92.4\% human accuracy and a range of 28.7--61.5\% for the evaluated frontier models on its hard setup. It also describes problems with questions that admit multiple correct answers.

This matters for Vericult. A cultural practice can have regional or situational alternatives. The verifier should not reject a response only because it differs from the first source found. Vericult therefore asks a second question about scope and variation.

CulturalBench is primarily a question-answering benchmark. Vericult is not evaluated on the same published questions under the same protocol. Its scores must not be compared numerically with CulturalBench accuracy.

\section{Social Norm Adaptation: NormAd}

NormAd distinguishes between recognizing a social rule and applying it when the context is incomplete \parencite{rao2025normad}. Its NormAd-Eti benchmark contains approximately 2,600 social-etiquette situations from 75 countries. The paper reports that even with explicit relevant norms, the best evaluated models scored below 82\% while humans scored above 95\%. With more abstract values and country information, model performance was below 60\%, while human performance remained above 90\%.

These results illustrate why a prompt that only names a country may be insufficient for a confident cultural judgment. Vericult first extracts explicit prompt context and includes an abstention option when the evidence is insufficient. The two systems nevertheless answer different questions: NormAd tests social acceptability judgments, while Vericult assesses generated answers.

\section{Cultural Adaptation of Generators: CultureLLM}

CultureLLM addresses cultural differences through training data augmentation and model adaptation \parencite{li2024culturellm}. The authors use World Values Survey seed data and train culturally adapted models. The NeurIPS 2024 paper reports experiments across 60 culture-related datasets and nine cultures. This is a generator-side approach: the aim is to make model outputs better before they are produced.

Vericult works at a different point in the system. It does not train or change the generator. It receives a fixed prompt and response after generation. CultureLLM is useful background, but it is not a direct baseline.

\section{Reward Models and RewardBench}

Reward models usually assign a scalar value to a response. In pairwise evaluation, a higher score is treated as evidence that one answer is preferred. RewardBench evaluates reward models using chosen/rejected response pairs across tasks such as chat, safety, and reasoning \parencite{lambert2025rewardbench}. The benchmark highlights that reward-model performance depends on the kinds of comparisons included.

M-RewardBench extends reward-model evaluation to multilingual settings \parencite{gureja2025mrewardbench}. Its published ACL 2025 study contains 2,870 preference instances across 23 languages and reports gaps between English and non-English performance. This is relevant because a high general reward score does not establish that a response handles local culture correctly.

In the present thesis, Skywork is an independent comparator. Its raw score is not evidence for any particular factual or cultural claim, and it does not enter Vericult's scoring pipeline. A raw reward score also does not automatically have a calibrated threshold for cultural appropriateness. Any threshold or transformation must be declared before comparing results.

\section{CARB: Cultural Awareness in Reward Models}

The closest methodological motivation is the Cultural Awareness Reward modeling Benchmark (CARB), proposed by Zhang et al.\ \parencite{zhang2025carb}. The arXiv paper, first posted in September 2025, describes ten cultures and four cultural domains. It evaluates reward models on culture-related preferences and reports that some models rely on surface-level correlations rather than the cultural meaning of a response. The authors also propose Think-as-Locals to improve culture-aware reward modeling.

CARB motivates part of the problem, but Vericult does not use CARB's architecture. It is not a hybrid of CARB and a reward model. Vericult selects response targets, gathers evidence for the checkable ones, freezes that evidence, and then applies its rubric.

The difference in evaluation units is important. CARB studies reward-model preference behavior. Vericult's final experiment consists of 360 individual prompt--response pairs. No CARB score can be used as a direct head-to-head performance number unless both approaches are tested on a shared evaluation task with a common independent reference.

\section{Direct LLM Judges and Evaluation Bias}

Zheng et al.\ study LLM-based judges in MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena \parencite{zheng2023judging}. Wang et al.\ show that changing the order of responses can alter comparative judgments, a problem known as position bias \parencite{wang2024fair}. These studies motivate careful prompt design and clear separation of comparison systems.

Vericult's final experiment evaluates one response at a time, so it does not have response-order bias within a Best-of-4 choice. It can still suffer from semantic judge bias, cultural blind spots, or overly confident reasoning. Its evidence stage and diagnostic traces are intended to make such errors easier to investigate, not to eliminate them automatically.

\section{Pluralism and the Limits of External Evidence}

Sorensen et al.\ argue for pluralistic alignment that recognizes disagreement and variation in human preferences \parencite{sorensen2024pluralistic}. This matters because cultural appropriateness is sometimes a factual question and sometimes a question about acceptable choices in a specific relationship or community.

For example, an institutional rule can be checked against an official source. A statement about what all members of a community prefer cannot normally be verified in the same way. The second claim may require a narrower description, acknowledgement of diversity, or abstention. Vericult's evidence memo therefore records scope and variation, not only whether a source contains a matching phrase.

\section{Intercultural Pragmatics}

Culture also affects how language is used in a situation. Kecskes describes intercultural pragmatics as the study of language use between people who bring different linguistic and cultural backgrounds into the same interaction \parencite{kecskes2014intercultural}. This matters for Vericult because a sentence can be grammatically correct but still use the wrong register, form of address, level of directness, or regional expression.

This supports a separate language and pragmatics dimension in the rubric. It also supports the use of prompt context. A greeting, joke, request, or disagreement cannot always be judged without knowing who is speaking, where the interaction takes place, and what relationship the speakers have.

\section{Human Disagreement Is Not Always Noise}

Cultural judgments can be subjective. Plank argues that disagreement between annotators is often treated as annotation noise even when several answers are reasonable \parencite{plank2022labelvariation}. This is relevant for culture, values, identity, and etiquette.

Inter-annotator agreement is still useful because it shows how consistently people apply a labeling task. Artstein and Poesio review common agreement measures and explain why the choice of measure depends on the annotation setup \parencite{artstein2008agreement}. Krippendorff gives a broader framework for reliability in content analysis and for handling different measurement scales \parencite{krippendorff2018content}.

For this thesis, disagreement has two consequences. First, the old five-person pilot is not treated as perfect ground truth. Second, the final verifier is allowed to abstain when the available basis is too weak. A low-agreement human case should not be hidden behind a single majority label without discussion.

\section{Abstention and Selective Prediction}

Abstention is also supported by work on selective classification. Geifman and El-Yaniv study classifiers that can reject uncertain cases instead of forcing a prediction for every input \parencite{geifman2017selective}. Their setting is different from cultural verification, but the basic idea is useful here: a system can trade coverage for more reliable decisions.

Vericult does not copy their method. It uses a simpler rule. If the evidence needed for a cultural dimension is unresolved, that dimension can abstain. The public score is then suppressed instead of presenting a precise number that would hide the uncertainty.
\section{Research Gap and Scope of the Contribution}

The contribution is not that no previous research has evaluated culture, used retrieval, or designed rubrics. The narrower gap addressed here is an inspectable post-hoc pipeline for one fixed response that combines explicit cultural dimensions, selected externally checkable targets, neutral verification questions, candidate-blind evidence collection after question generation, frozen evidence memos, abstention, and replayable traces.

This is a methodological contribution. Whether it leads to better cultural decisions is an empirical question. The final experiment and its independent comparison systems are designed to study that question without assuming a positive outcome.
