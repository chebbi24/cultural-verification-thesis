Large language models are used for information, advice, writing, and decision support in many cultural settings. A response can sound correct and still be culturally inappropriate. It may treat a regional habit as a national rule, confuse etiquette with law, ignore religious or historical sensitivity, or describe a group through a stereotype. These cases are hard to evaluate because culture depends on context and because people within the same culture do not always agree.

This thesis presents \textbf{Vericult}, a standalone verifier for cultural appropriateness in LLM responses. Vericult checks one prompt--response pair at a time. It first extracts only context that is stated in the prompt and selects relevant dimensions from a ten-part rubric. It then selects up to three important response targets. Targets that can be checked externally are turned into two neutral questions. The evidence stage searches for sources, records scope and variation, and freezes the final evidence memo before the response is compared with it. The final stage scores the relevant cultural dimensions and can abstain when the evidence is too weak. The full process is stored in a trace.

The final dataset contains 360 fixed prompt--response pairs. It has three equal parts: 120 custom PLT red-team prompts created in this project, 120 prompts selected from six public datasets, and 120 new red-team prompts grounded in external public sources. The last group is called ExternalRedteam120 in the thesis and \texttt{redteam120} in the repository. All 360 responses are generated with the same frozen GPT-OSS 120B generator. Vericult uses a separate Qwen3.6 35B-A3B backbone. Evidence is collected in LIVE mode, audited and hashed, and then reused in REPLAY mode. REPLAY is the main Vericult run reported in the thesis.

The contribution is not a claim that web evidence can define one correct culture. The contribution is a verification process that separates context, evidence, uncertainty, and final judgment. This makes the result easier to inspect than a single reward or judge score. The thesis studies both the strengths and the limits of this approach on the frozen 360-pair dataset.
